# AI-Writing Pattern Catalog

Use this catalog to diagnose clustered tells. A single word, punctuation mark, formal construction, or polished sentence is weak evidence by itself.

## Content Patterns

1. **Inflated significance**: replace claims about legacy, pivotal moments, broader trends, and enduring impact with the concrete fact.
2. **Notability lists**: replace publication or follower lists with one relevant sourced example.
3. **Superficial `-ing` analysis**: remove participial tails that add symbolism or consequence without evidence.
4. **Promotional language**: replace `vibrant`, `renowned`, `groundbreaking`, `breathtaking`, and similar sales language with observable detail.
5. **Vague attribution**: name the source for `experts`, `observers`, and `reports`, or keep the claim explicitly uncertain.
6. **Formulaic challenges and outlook**: replace generic obstacle and future sections with dated events, actors, and actions.

## Language And Grammar Patterns

7. **AI vocabulary clusters**: simplify repeated words such as `crucial`, `delve`, `foster`, `landscape`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry`, and `underscore`.
8. **Copula avoidance**: prefer `is`, `are`, and `has` over `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, and `features` when meaning is unchanged.
9. **Negative parallelism**: state the positive claim directly instead of `not only`, `not just`, or clipped tailing negations.
10. **Forced triads**: use the number of items the subject actually requires.
11. **Synonym cycling**: repeat the precise noun instead of rotating synonyms for the same entity.
12. **False ranges**: list unrelated endpoints directly instead of presenting them as `from X to Y`.
13. **Hidden actors**: restore the actor when passive voice or a subjectless fragment obscures responsibility.

## Style Patterns

14. **Em and en dashes**: an isolated dash is not evidence by itself, but replace every em and en dash in the final rewrite with punctuation or sentence structure that fits the context.
15. **Mechanical boldface**: retain emphasis only when the document's real hierarchy needs it.
16. **Inline-header lists**: turn repetitive bold-label bullets into prose or a genuine table when comparison matters.
17. **Title Case headings**: use sentence case unless the source style requires another convention.
18. **Decorative emoji**: remove emoji that only decorates headings or bullets.
19. **Quotation-style drift**: preserve the source or house quote style instead of switching styles mechanically.

## Communication Patterns

20. **Chat residue**: remove `I hope this helps`, `let me know`, `would you like`, and similar correspondence pasted into content.
21. **Knowledge-cutoff filler**: state what is known, state a genuine gap briefly, or omit the unsupported passage. Leave plausible biography and motive out of evidence-based prose.
22. **Servile agreement**: replace praise and excessive agreement with the relevant substance.

## Filler And Framing Patterns

23. **Filler phrases**: compress `in order to`, `due to the fact that`, `at this point in time`, and similar padding.
24. **Stacked hedging**: keep only the uncertainty level supported by the source.
25. **Generic positive conclusions**: end with a concrete result, commitment, date, or next action.
26. **Predicate hyphenation**: keep normal attributive compounds but drop mechanical hyphens after the noun when standard usage does.
27. **Authority tropes**: replace `the real question`, `at its core`, and `what really matters` with the actual claim.
28. **Announcements**: remove `let's dive in`, `here's what you need to know`, and other previews of the next sentence.
29. **Fragmented headers**: remove the one-line warm-up that merely repeats its heading.
30. **Diff-anchored prose**: describe the current system unless the document is inherently historical or version-scoped.
31. **Manufactured punchlines**: combine stacked short dramatic fragments into natural rhythm.
32. **Aphorism formulas**: replace `X is the language, currency, or architecture of Y` with the concrete relationship.
33. **Fake-candid openers**: remove theatrical `Honestly?`, `Here's the thing`, and `Real talk` hooks when they introduce an ordinary point.

## False Positives

Preserve these when they fit the source: polished grammar, technical vocabulary, ordinary transition words, legitimate curly quotes, one em dash in otherwise human prose, a single emphatic sentence, unsourced but clearly framed opinion, established templates, and mixed formal and casual register.

Prefer evidence clusters: repeated promotional diction plus forced triads plus generic conclusions is meaningful; one occurrence usually is not.
